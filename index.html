Education System

How subjects, books and exams became one connected system on Aladia.

Overview

Before I designed subjects, books or exams one by one, I had to solve the problem they shared: all teaching content was trapped inside courses. I designed one structure for all three, shared libraries under Education, with the same rules, linked into courses and lectures.

Subjects, books and exams were each built inside courses, and copied into every new one. Before designing any of them, I designed one structure for all three: shared libraries above the course, with the same rules.

UI/UX designer, course domain · Aladia · 2024–2026

3libraries under one Education group, with the same structure

How I worked
  1. 01Discover

    Saw the same pattern three times: subjects, books and exams were all built inside courses, and copied into every new one.

  2. 02Define

    The problem wasn’t one feature. It was where content lived. It had to move above the course.

  3. 03Structure

    Designed one model for all three: a shared library, an item inside it, and links into courses and lectures.

  4. 04Rules

    Gave the three the same four roles and the same behaviour, so teachers learn the system once.

  5. 05Design

    Designed each part end to end: Subject Areas, Book Libraries and Exam Collections.

  6. 06Connect

    Linked them together, so adding a subject to a course can bring its teacher, books and exams with it.

01 — Context and problemEverything lived inside the course

On Aladia, a course was a container for everything: its subjects, its books and later its exams were all created inside it. That works for one course. It breaks for a school running dozens.

  • Subjects were rebuilt in every course. Mathematics in one course had nothing to do with Mathematics in the next, and a teacher with the same subject in 8 courses was invited 8 times.
  • Books were files attached to single lectures. One textbook used in 12 places meant 12 uploads, and 12 places to update.
  • Exams didn’t exist yet. Built the same way, every course would have had its own copy of the same exam.
Three features, one structural problem: content that should be shared was trapped inside courses.

02 — My role and goalStructure first, screens second

I was the UI/UX designer on the team responsible for the course domain, briefed to improve the learning flow and materials for students and teachers. I designed the overall structure first, then each part in depth: I redesigned Subjects, and designed Books and Exams from scratch. I worked closely with the project manager, the backend and frontend developers and the other designers, from requirements to handoff, and built working prototypes of my designs in code with Claude Code.

The goal: every piece of teaching content lives once, and every course uses it.

03 — DecisionContent lives once, courses point to it

Rejected
Fix each feature on its own, with faster copying or bulk editing. The copies would still drift apart.
Chosen
Lift all teaching content above the course, into shared libraries. A course doesn’t own its subjects, books and exams anymore; it uses them.

That gave every piece of content the same shape: a library owned by an organisation or a freelance teacher, items inside it, and links from courses and lectures that decide how each item is used there: a level, a page range, a date, a grading scale.

All three libraries sit together under Education, in settings and in the navigation, and follow the same structure. This is the map I designed before any screen.

01 — ContextSchools, universities and companies on one platform

Aladia is an e-learning platform used by very different institutions. An Italian liceo grades from 18 to 30, a corporate course from 0 to 100. Teachers needed to create exams, run them with supervision and grade them, all inside the platform.

02 — The problemExams didn’t exist, and the obvious design would break

  • Teachers reuse the same exam in several courses, and may supervise forty students at once in a live exam.
  • Students take exams from home, monitored, where a bad WiFi connection can look like cheating.
  • Most platforms put exams inside courses. Aladia had already hit that with Books and Subjects: every course got its own copy, and the copies drifted apart.
The hard part wasn’t the exam. It was keeping one exam consistent across many courses, grading rules and students.

03 — My role and goalFrom the structure to the last error state

I was the UI/UX designer on the team responsible for the course domain. I designed the exam system end to end: the structure, the builder with AI, live proctoring, grading and the student side, for web and mobile, and prototyped every flow in Figma. I worked closely with the project manager, the backend and frontend developers and the other designers, from requirements to handoff, and built working prototypes of my designs in code with Claude Code.

The goal: write an exam once, run it anywhere, and keep the teacher in control, of the questions, the grade and every flag.

04 — Decision 1The exam lives outside the course

Solves: one exam turning into many drifting copies.

Rejected
Exams inside courses, like most platforms. Fast to build, but five courses would mean five copies to maintain.
Chosen
Exam Collections, outside any course. An exam is written once, and each course adds its own date, rules and grading scale.

05 — Decision 2The course decides the grade, not the exam

Solves: the same exam running in courses graded 18–30, A+ to F or pass/fail.

Rejected
Save the grading scale inside the exam. Every institution would need its own copy again.
Chosen
The exam only knows raw points. Each course sets its scale, pass mark, weights and which attempt counts.

06 — Decision 3Supervising 40 students without watching 40 screens

Solves: one teacher, forty students at home, and no way to watch forty feeds.

Rejected
One shared room for everyone. Students would see each other, and the teacher would get one long recording.
Chosen
One breakout room per student, in one grid, created automatically, with rooms that need attention lighting up.

The system flags, the human decides. A lost camera, a WiFi drop or leaving fullscreen each gets its own calm message, and is logged for the teacher. Nothing ends an exam automatically.

Design System

Overview

Aladia had a design kit, but the platform had many domains, and Figma files and the product had drifted apart. I built a design system from scratch in Figma, variables first: a core palette, themes, breakpoint modes and components on top, plus a branching workflow so the team could use it without breaking it.

Component library

Aladia’s design kit had drifted away from the product, and teams weren’t aligned. I built a design system from scratch in Figma, variables first, so a value changed in one place updates the whole product.

Product designer · Aladia · 2024–2026

735design tokens across three tiers
450+components built on the tokens
3 + 2breakpoint modes and Light/Dark themes

01 — The problemOne kit, many domains, no single truth

Aladia already had a design kit. But the platform was big, with many domains, and over time the Figma files and the live product drifted apart. Teams were not aligned on which version was right.

The goal: one source of truth, so we could design faster and stay consistent.
  • Decisions made once, reused everywhere. Change a value in one place, and the whole product follows.
  • Built from scratch in Figma, variables first, then components built on top of them.

02 — StructureFour layers, each built on the one before

STRUCTURE
CoreTier 1 · raw values, no meaning yet
→
ThemesTier 2 · names by purpose, Light and Dark
→
BreakpointsDesktop, Tablet and Mobile modes
→
Componentsatoms, molecules, organisms

Each layer only uses the one before it, so a change at the bottom flows all the way up.

03 — Tier 1 · CoreThe raw palette

Over 400 variables for colour, typography, radius, spacing, shadows and more. None of them has a meaning yet: they are just the values the rest of the system is allowed to use.

04 — Tier 2 · ThemesNames by purpose

Themes give each value a job, like a primary background or a hover state. The same token points to a different core value in Light and in Dark, so components never need to know which theme they are in.

05 — BreakpointsOne component, three screen sizes

Breakpoint modes for Desktop, Tablet and Mobile change values like spacing, sizes and visibility. A component adapts by switching mode, without being redrawn for each screen. This is what made every screen on the platform responsive, with mobile versions designed too.

06 — ComponentsBuilt in atomic levels, with variants and states

  • Atoms: foundation pieces with one job each, like dividers, logos, icons and images.
  • Molecules: small interactive parts, like buttons, dropdowns, input fields and chips.
  • Organisms: complex, complete sections, like tables, breadcrumbs, cards and pop-ups.

07 — WorkflowHow the team worked with it

WORKFLOW
Domain filesone Figma file per domain, with a local Components page
→
Branch per taska Figma branch named after the task, then design
→
Approve and mergeafter approval the branch is merged

The system stays clean, because nothing reaches it without review.

01 — ContextConnecting Odoo to everything else

Connecta links Odoo with apps like Shopify, Bol.com, Exact Online and Magento, so businesses can sync orders, products and stock without writing code.

Two kinds of people use it. Business owners just want to know that everything is working. Developers and admins need enough detail to fix what breaks, fast.

02 — The problemA time-saving tool that felt like it needed a developer

Behind every connection are mappings, schedules, retries and conditions. The product showed all of it to everyone, all the time. I audited the whole product and found three symptoms:

  • Four separate dashboards in separate tabs (historical, technical, one for business owners and one for partners) showing overlapping data. Nobody got one clear answer.
  • Connecting Shopify needed the flow builder, the most advanced tool. A customer’s very first task was the hardest one.
  • The setup form was so long it broke its own layout.
The problem wasn’t too many features. Simple and advanced tasks were mixed together, and everyone saw everything.

03 — My role and goalOne designer, the whole product

I was the only designer, working directly with the co-founders. I audited the product, framed the problem, designed 50+ screens across 20 flows, and rebuilt the final UI as a responsive coded prototype. The setup wizard had a hard deadline: it had to ship before a trade fair.

The goal: keep all the power, but show the simple path by default. Each person sees what their role needs, and simple tasks never need the advanced tools.

04 — Decision 1Two views of the same data

Solves: four overlapping dashboards that worked for no one.

Rejected
One dashboard, with explanations for owners. Owners would still dig through errors and settings they don’t need.
Chosen
One dashboard with a role switcher, and two views over the same data: business owners and partners merged into Management, historical and technical merged into Development.

A constraint: ideally the view would follow the user’s role automatically, from the backend. The client couldn’t support that yet, so I designed a simple switcher at the top, which still turned four tabs into one place.

When an app can’t retry, the button is disabled, not hidden, so the limit is visible. My rule: hide what your role can’t do, disable what the system can’t do.

05 — Decision 2Taking setup out of the flow builder

Solves: a first connection that required the most advanced tool.

Rejected
Keep setup in the flow builder. New users would have to learn the whole platform before one connection.
Chosen
A separate four-step wizard that starts from the app library.

Concept project. A self-initiated exercise, designed from brief to developer handover. No real client or users, so there are no results to report.

01 — The brief500 channels, one remote

The brief described a live TV platform for Android TV with more than 500 channels, some free, some premium and some locked. The existing programme guide (EPG) was a wall of data: no hierarchy, no recommendations and no clear way to tell what the viewer could actually watch.

  • Choice paralysis: scrolling hundreds of channels before choosing anything.
  • Entitlement: showing what is free, premium or locked without cluttering the screen.
  • Input: everything had to work with a D-Pad remote only, at 1920×1080, read from a sofa.

02 — The approachBroadcast guide meets streaming app

Rejected
A classic EPG grid only. Familiar, but it is exactly the wall of data that causes the problem.
Rejected
A streaming-style home with rails only. Easy to browse, but it hides what is on now across 500 channels.
Chosen
A hybrid layout, with a full-bleed hero panel, a curated “Recommended Live Now” rail and a smart EPG grid on the same screen.

The hero panel updates on every D-Pad move. Focusing a programme anywhere shows its full title, time and actions at the top. This solves long titles on TV without tooltips or hover states, which a remote doesn’t have.

03 — EntitlementNever colour alone

Every programme is Free, Premium or Locked. Each state is shown three ways at once (colour, opacity and a text label), so it still reads for colour-blind viewers and from across the room. Pressing OK on a free or premium programme plays it; on a locked one it opens the upgrade modal instead.

✓ Free★ Premium🔒 Locked

Every interactive element also has a visible focused state, because on TV the focus is the cursor.

04 — Logic flowEvery path, not just the happy one

Before any screen, I mapped the whole flow: loading with a skeleton, data errors with retry, the hero actions (Watch Live, Info, Restart), the restart sheet for programmes that started earlier, and locked channels leading to the upgrade modal or back to the guide.

05 — Design systemSame structure, different screen

The design system uses the same two first tiers as my Aladia work: Core Tier 1 primitives and Themes Tier 2 semantic tokens, sized for TV with minimum font sizes and spacing for 10-foot viewing. See how I build design systems →

06 — HandoverWritten for developers

The handover covers how each behaviour should work: how the hero updates on focus, how the rail and grid scroll, where the “now” line sits, when long titles start to scroll, which way BACK goes, and how often data refreshes.
