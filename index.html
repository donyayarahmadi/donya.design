Education System

Before I designed subjects, books or exams one by one, I had to solve the problem they shared: all teaching content was trapped inside courses. I designed one structure for all three, shared libraries under Education, with the same rules, linked into courses and lectures.

01 — The problemEverything lived inside the course

On Aladia, a course was a container for everything. Its subjects, its books and later its exams were all created inside it. That works for one course. It breaks for a school running dozens of them.

  • Subjects were rebuilt in every course. Mathematics in one course had nothing to do with Mathematics in the next, and a teacher with the same subject in 8 courses was invited 8 times.
  • Books were files attached to single lectures. One textbook used in 12 places meant 12 uploads, and 12 places to update.
  • Exams didn’t exist yet. Built the same way, every course would have had its own copy of the same exam.
Three features, one structural problem: content that should be shared was trapped inside courses.

Who it affected

  • Academic coordinators, managing 12 courses and more than 50 subjects, repeating every change course by course.
  • Teachers, keeping copies of the same book or exam up to date by hand.
  • Students, receiving a 300-page PDF and a message saying which chapter to read.

02 — The ideaContent lives once, courses point to it

Rejected
Fix each feature on its own, with faster copying or bulk editing. The copies would still drift apart.
Chosen
Lift all teaching content above the course, into shared libraries. A course doesn’t own its subjects, books and exams anymore; it uses them.

That gave every piece of content the same shape: a library owned by an organisation or a freelance teacher, items inside it, and links from courses and lectures that decide how each item is used there: a level, a page range, a date, a grading scale.

05 — Shared rulesLearn it once, use it everywhere

  • The same four roles (Owner, Curator, Editor, Viewer) in every library, so permissions work the same way for subjects, books and exams.
  • Hide what your role can’t do. If someone can’t edit, the action isn’t there, instead of appearing disabled with an error.
  • One question at a time. When content is linked to a course, related things are offered, never added silently: “This subject has books. Add them too?”
  • The course decides how content is used. The exam has no date or grading scale of its own; the book has no page range. The course and lecture set them.

Exam Module

Designing a complete exam system, from the first question to the final grade.

Overview

Aladia had no exam system: no builder, no proctoring, no grading. I designed the whole thing from a blank canvas: three exam formats, AI-assisted question creation, live proctored exams, and a grading model that works across courses with completely different scoring scales.

01 — The problemExams didn’t exist yet

Teachers on Aladia needed to create exams, run them with supervision and grade them, all inside the platform. None of that existed yet.

And Aladia serves very different institutions. An Italian liceo grades from 18 to 30, while a corporate course grades from 0 to 100. The same system had to work for both.

Who it affected

  • Teachers often reuse the same exam in several courses, and may supervise forty students at once during a live exam.
  • Students take exams from home, with camera, microphone and screen monitored, where a bad WiFi connection can look like cheating.

How I found the problem

Most LMS platforms put exams inside courses. It’s the obvious structure, and it ships faster.

But Aladia had already hit this problem twice, with Books and with Subjects. When content lived inside a course, every course ended up with its own copy. Fix a typo in one, and the others still have it. Over time, the copies stopped being the same thing at all.

The hardest part wasn’t the exam itself. It was keeping one exam consistent across many courses, many grading rules and many students.

02 — Decision 1The exam lives outside the course

Rejected
Exams inside courses, like most LMS platforms. Simple and fast to build, but a teacher using the same final exam in five courses would end up with five copies to maintain.
Chosen
Exam Collections at the profile level. An exam is created once and used anywhere.

Each course adds its own schedule, proctoring rules and grading scale on top, without ever touching the exam itself. The exam only knows raw points. Each course decides how those points become a grade.

Why it matters: the same exam can run in a course graded 18–30 and one graded 0–100 on the same afternoon. And a teacher with forty students across three courses maintains exactly one version of each exam.

04 — Decision 2The course decides the grade, not the exam

Every institution grades differently. An Italian liceo grades from 18 to 30, a school might use A+ to F, and a corporate course may only need pass or fail. And because exams live outside courses, the same exam can appear in all of them.

Rejected
Save the grading scale inside the exam. Every institution would need its own copy, which is the exact problem Decision 1 solved.
Rejected
One fixed scale for the whole platform. Simple, but wrong for most institutions.
Chosen
The exam stays neutral and only knows raw points. Each course decides how points become a grade.

What I designed: three settings inside each course.

  • Grading scale: the course sets its maximum score (30, 10, 100…) and pass percentage, and exam points scale automatically. Advanced grading adds custom levels like A+ to F.
  • Weights per exam type: a Final can count far more than a Quiz.
  • Which attempt counts: Highest by default, because it rewards improvement without punishing a student for trying again. A stricter course can choose Latest or Average.

Why it works: none of these settings touch the exam. Changing a course’s scale rescales every exam in it instantly, without editing a single question.

05 — Decision 3Supervising 40 students without watching 40 screens

On exam day, one teacher may supervise forty students at once, each at home with a camera and a shared screen. Nobody can watch forty video feeds continuously.

Rejected
One shared room for everyone. Students could see or hear each other, and the teacher would get one long recording to scroll through.
Rejected
A simple list of student names. The teacher would have to click into each student to check on them.
Chosen
One breakout room per student, shown in a grid, with the rooms that need attention highlighted.
  • The rooms are created automatically from the participant list, so the teacher sets up nothing on the day.
  • Monitor Exam shows one room per student: webcam and exam screen side by side.
  • Rooms are sorted into tabs: Open, Closed (students who submit move out of the way automatically) and Proctoring alerts.
  • A room lights up the moment something needs attention, like a raised hand or a flag.
A teacher can’t scan forty rooms, but they can react to the two or three that light up. And each student’s session is recorded separately, so every attempt has its own file.

Small decisions that remove work

  • If the exam is longer than its calendar slot, the end time extends automatically, instead of cutting the student’s time or showing an error.
  • The entry window (how long students have to start) grows with the exam’s length, so the teacher never has to set it.
  • When grading, AI suggests a score for open questions, and the teacher accepts or changes it.

06 — Decision 4The system flags, the human decides

Students take exams from home, with camera, microphone and screen monitored. In that situation, a technical problem can look like cheating. A student whose WiFi drops shouldn’t be treated as suspicious.

Rejected
End the exam automatically when a violation is detected. Too harsh, and often wrong.
Rejected
Show one generic error message. Confusing for the student, and it reads as suspicious behaviour.
Chosen
Flag and notify, never end. The student is told in the moment, the incident is logged, and the teacher decides.

What I designed: a specific, calm state for each failure.

  • Camera or microphone disconnected → prompt to reconnect
  • WiFi drops → the exam pauses and shows that it’s reconnecting
  • Leaving fullscreen (Esc or F11) → prompt to return to the exam

Naming exactly what happened protects the student as much as it protects the exam. The same thinking runs through the whole student experience:

  • Scoped rechecks: students select the specific questions they want reviewed and explain why, so a recheck doesn’t become a second full grading.
  • Controlled chat: students can reach the teacher mid-exam, but only for clarification and support.
  • Oral exams got their own one-to-one flow, instead of a video call attached to a written exam.

07 — PerformanceOne number teachers can trust

A teacher with several courses may have a hundred students or more. Exam results alone don’t show who needs help, and neither does attendance. A student can attend every class and fail every exam; another can skip classes and still score well, until the material gets harder.

What I designed: Performance, one number that combines both signals: Attendance 30% + Exams 70%.

Rejected
Let each course set its own formula, like grading. A “75” in one course would mean something different from a “75” in another, and nobody could compare.
Chosen
One fixed formula everywhere, so the number always means the same thing.

Exams carry the larger share on purpose: attendance shows engagement, exams show what a student can actually do, and engagement shouldn’t outweigh competence.

01 — The problemBuilt once, rebuilt everywhere

Subjects lived inside courses. Every new course meant creating Mathematics, Italian and History again from scratch, and the Mathematics in one course had nothing to do with the Mathematics in the next. Books and exams couldn’t be linked to a subject at all.

Teachers were stuck in the same pattern. They could only be assigned to a subject inside a single course, so a teacher with Mathematics in eight courses had to be invited eight separate times. Once inside, they faced a calendar showing every lecture of a year-long course, while they taught roughly one lesson a week. Finding their own lectures was hard.

Who it affected

The person who felt this most was the academic coordinator, managing 12 courses and more than 50 subjects. Every change had to be repeated course by course. When a teacher went on leave, replacing them meant redoing the same work in every course they taught.

“I have Mathematics in 8 different courses. When a teacher goes on leave and I need to replace him everywhere, I have to do it 8 times.”Academic coordinator

The insight

A subject isn’t a course-level idea. Mathematics is Mathematics wherever it’s taught. The teacher is the same person, and the books and exams are the same resources.

So the subject should live above the course, and every course should simply use it.

02 — Decision 1Move subjects out of courses

Rejected
Keep subjects inside courses, and make copying them faster. Every copy would still drift apart, and the coordinator’s problem would stay.
Chosen
One shared Subject Area for each organisation or freelance teacher. Create a subject once, assign its teacher, link books and exams, then add it to any course, and everything comes with it.

Creating a subject is deliberately light: a title, a teacher, an icon and a colour. Subjects can also be organised by study level, like Mathematics for the first two years of a Liceo Scientifico, which matches how Italian schools actually teach.

One permission model everywhere: Subject Areas use the same four roles as Book Libraries and Exam Collections (Owner, Curator, Editor, Viewer), so teachers learn it once. If someone can’t do something because of their role, the action simply isn’t there, instead of appearing disabled with an error.

03 — Decision 2One decision about who teaches what

A school may run dozens of courses, and the same teacher often teaches the same subject in many of them. The coordinator had to invite each teacher to each course by hand, and keep track of who was where.

The new rule: the coordinator assigns a teacher to a subject once, and the platform works out which courses they need to be in.

  • A subject is added to a course: its teacher is invited automatically. I changed this from an optional invite to a mandatory one, because a course teaching Mathematics without its Mathematics teacher is broken.
  • The subject has no teacher yet: it still joins the course, so the coordinator isn’t blocked while staffing is being decided.
  • A teacher is assigned later: the system shows every course that uses the subject and offers to invite the teacher to all of them at once.

This also solves the coordinator’s leave problem: assigning a new teacher to the subject reaches every course that uses it. One change instead of eight.

04 — Decision 3Books and exams follow the subject

Once subjects lived outside courses, books and exams could be linked to them too. So adding a subject to a course could bring its content along.

Rejected
Add everything automatically. Fast, but teachers would lose control over their own course.
Chosen
Ask one clear question at a time: “This subject has books. Do you want to add them to the course?” The same for exams. Nothing is added without the teacher’s say.

The same logic reaches down to lectures: when a subject with books is assigned to a lecture, the teacher can add all of them, choose some, or skip. A fully built subject, with its teacher, books and exams, can now be added to a new course in one action.

05 — Decision 4One home for education content

Subject Areas, Book Libraries and Exam Collections work exactly the same way: reusable, linked, with the same roles.

Rejected
Three separate places in the navigation, one for each.
Chosen
One Education tab, where teachers land once and branch into subjects, books or exams.

“I have the same PDF uploaded 12 times across my courses. When I update it, I always forget at least one.”Department head with over 200 teaching PDFs

How I found the problem

I audited the existing workflow, mapped with the PM how books relate to subjects, teachers and courses, and watched teachers try to share one book across three courses. Every time, they uploaded a new copy.

Rejected
A better upload button. It would make uploading faster, but teachers would still create copies.
Chosen
Make books real objects on the platform, living in a central library with their own details, subjects and team roles. Create once, link to any course.

This meant designing a new data model before any screen, following the same structure as Subject Areas and Exam Collections: library → book → course → lecture → page range.

02 — Decision 1One place, one set of rules

Books, subjects and exams all work in a similar way and connect to each other. If each had its own place and its own rules, teachers would have to learn three systems.

Rejected
A separate section for books, with its own permissions.
Chosen
Book Libraries in the Education tab, next to Subject Areas and Exam Collections, with the same four roles: Owner, Curator, Editor and Viewer.

Why: a teacher who understands one understands the others. And when someone can’t do something because of their role, the action simply isn’t there, which makes the role system feel natural instead of bureaucratic.

03 — Decision 2Three paths, one form

Teachers don’t all have books in the same form. Some have a PDF, some can only point to a book for sale, and some write their own material. Add New Book opens with three paths that all lead to the same form:

  • Upload a PDF: the most common case.
  • Attach a purchase link: for copyrighted books that can’t be shared as files. The teacher adds the ISBN and a link, and students see exactly which book to buy instead of nothing.
  • Create it manually: for teachers writing their own material.

Three small decisions that removed friction

  • Cover images: asking teachers to make a cover for every book would stall the flow. The system shows the first five pages of the PDF, and the teacher picks one as the cover with a single tap.
  • Writing a book: instead of a new editor just for books, I reused the Document Builder, the same editor used for exams. AI sits inline on each block, like in Notion, not in a separate mode, so the teacher stays the author.
  • Book details: teachers can type them in, or search by ISBN and have everything fill in automatically. Most teachers already know the ISBN of their books, so this became the most used route: 48% of books were added this way.

04 — Decision 3Let the connections come to the teacher

A book belongs to a subject, and a subject can have its own teacher. When adding a book to a course, teachers had to remember all these connections, and they usually didn’t.

Rejected
Leave it to the teacher to add the related subject and invite the right people by hand.
Chosen
Ask at the right moment, with one question at a time the teacher can accept or skip.
  • “This book belongs to a subject. Should that subject be added to the course too?”
  • “That subject has a teacher. Should they be invited?”

Page ranges per lecture: a textbook covers a whole year, but one lecture covers a few pages. So the teacher sets a page range for each lecture: chapter three this week, chapter four next. The book lives in the library once, and each lecture points to a different part of it.

05 — What the student seesThe right reading, already in the lecture

All of this structure leads to one simple result for the student: the lecture page. Every book assigned to the lecture appears there, and opening it launches the built-in reader directly at the assigned pages.

Rejected
Before: a 300-page PDF and a separate message saying which chapter to read.
Chosen
Now: the teacher’s decision arrives already applied.

What I learned

For something new, the concept has to come before the interface.

01 — The problem“No-code” doesn’t mean simple

Behind every connection there is data mapping, sync schedules, retries and conditions between systems that don’t naturally agree. The product showed all of this to everyone, all the time. A tool built to save people time made them feel they needed a developer.

Who it affected

  • Business owners want a quick answer: is everything working?
  • Developers and admins need enough detail to fix what’s broken, fast.

And for both of them, simply connecting a new app meant going through the full flow builder, the most complex part of the product.

How I found the problem

As the only designer, I worked across the whole product instead of one feature, so I could see the same pattern everywhere:

  • the same data appeared in three overlapping views, and none of them worked well for either user;
  • a simple task (connecting Shopify) required the most advanced tool in the product;
  • the setup form had so many fields that it broke the layout it lived in.
The problem wasn’t too many features. It was that simple and advanced tasks were mixed together, and everyone saw everything.

02 — Decision 1Two views of the same data

Rejected
One dashboard for everyone. It would do both jobs badly: too technical for owners, not detailed enough for developers.
Rejected
Keep the technical dashboard and add explanations for owners. Owners would still have to dig through errors and settings they don’t need.
Chosen
Two views over the same data, each built for one kind of user.
  • Management view is calm and read-only. It shows whether everything is connected and healthy, with nothing to configure.
  • Development view is built for the people doing the work. Repeating errors are grouped instead of flooding the list, records can be filtered, and every issue has retry, retry all and retry selected, plus a shortcut to the source record in Odoo.

When an app doesn’t support retries, the button appears disabled instead of hidden, so the limitation is visible instead of mysterious. My rule across products: hide what your role can’t do, disable what the system can’t do.

03 — Decision 2Taking setup out of the flow builder

Connecting a new app meant going through the full flow builder. It’s powerful, but far too much for someone who just wants to link Shopify and move on.

Rejected
Keep setup inside the flow builder. New users would have to understand the whole platform before creating a single connection.
Chosen
A separate four-step wizard that starts with searching the app library.

A problem I found along the way: the connection-details step had so many fields that it broke the wizard’s sidebar layout. I kept only the essential fields and moved the rest into a collapsed Advanced settings section. The main path stays short, and the depth is still there for anyone who needs it.

04 — Decision 3A canvas you can still read months later

Connecta Studio is where the real logic gets built: a canvas where people connect blocks to define how data moves between systems, how fields map and what conditions apply. As flows grew, the screen became crowded, and people had to find a flow, open it and understand it again, often weeks later.

Rejected
Open Studio as a full page, taking over the whole product. Users would lose their place every time they edited a flow.
Chosen
Open Studio as a slide-in overlay from a quieter button in the top bar, so people can edit a flow and return to where they were.
  • A Studio start page with Quick Create, recently closed tabs and a browse panel, so people get back to their work in one click.
  • Redesigned canvas blocks and navigation, so a flow stays readable as it grows.
  • In the mapping tool, a field picker with drill-down navigation, a back button and required-field markers, and more than 70 functions organised into categories instead of one long list.

Describe a flow, and AI drafts it

From the same start page, people can describe what they need in plain words. Connecta AI builds a draft flow, lists every step it created, and saves it in Studio, where it stays fully editable. The person can open it on the canvas or ask for a change.
